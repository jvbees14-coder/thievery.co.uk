How to play

Everyone can see the colour of every card on the table. Nobody but its owner can see the rank. Your job is to work out the ranks in front of you and name them out loud before anybody manages the same trick on you.

1. The deal

The deck is 26 cards — Ace to King in one red suit, Ace to King in one black suit — dealt out into three, four, five or six hands, as evenly as the table allows. There is no draw pile and nothing is held back: every card is in somebody’s row from the first move to the last.

The first few players — as many as there are hands — take one each. Anyone after that joins somebody already seated: the two of them share that hand, see exactly the same cards, and either can play it when their turn comes round. Two to a hand and no more, so six hands seat up to twelve people.

A hand nobody has taken can be dealt to the house instead, so one or two of you can still play a full table. Bots come at three levels — a Novice who only plays the colours, a Sharp one who reads the row, and a Ruthless one who counts the whole table — and they see no more of your cards than anybody else does. A bot hands its seat straight back when a real player turns up to want it.

2. Arrange your hand

You are dealt your cards face up — to you, and to nobody else. Drag them into ascending order, ace low, and press Lock in. Two cards of the same rank can go either way round, and an ace is wild in position: you may hide it anywhere in the row.

Once everyone has locked in, all the rows turn face down at once. Red cards lie sideways and black cards stand upright, so from now on the whole table knows the colour and the position of every card, and only you know what yours actually are.

3. Read the table

Because every row is in ascending order, a hidden card is fenced in by its neighbours. A face-down card sitting between a face-up 4 and a face-up 7 has to be a 4, 5, 6 or 7. Colour narrows it further: there is exactly one 9 of each colour in the deck, so once the black 9 has turned over anywhere on the table, no other black card is a 9. Every flip tightens the picture for everybody — including for the person who did the flipping.

4. Take your turn

Click any face-down card that is not yours and name a rank, from Ace to King.

  • Right: the card flips face up for the whole table — and you go again. A good run can empty somebody out in a single turn.
  • Wrong: nothing happens. You lose no cards and give nothing away beyond the guess itself; the turn simply passes to the next hand.

5. Win the round

The round ends the instant one hand has every card it could guess at face up — that is, every card belonging to everyone else. That hand wins, and both players win it if the hand is shared. Playing on your own account, that makes the winner the last person still holding anything face down.

Five and six hands: power-ups

The deck does not grow with the table. At six hands the same 26 cards are spread 5/5/4/4/4/4, so every row is four or five cards instead of six or seven — and a hidden card with almost no neighbours is barely fenced in at all. There is less to deduce, exactly when there are more people wanting to deduce it, and one hot streak can run through half the table before anybody else gets a turn.

So at five and six hands everybody draws a power-up at the start of each of their own turns — one per turn, never one per guess — and may carry three at a time. You never draw a second copy of something you are already holding. There is no switch for it: those two sizes are always dealt with them, and three and four hands never are. The house draws none.

The common ones buy information, and buy it for you alone: read one hidden card, or hear how many of a rank are still face down anywhere on the table. The rarer ones interrupt: trip the alarm ends whatever run is going on, and anybody may pull it at any time, including out of turn. That is the table’s way of stopping somebody six correct guesses deep.

Partnerships (four hands)

A four-hand table can be played as two teams instead: hands 1 and 3 against hands 2 and 4. You only guess against the hands opposite you, and at the start of your turn your partner may privately show you one of their face-down cards — or show you nothing. It is the one way to pass information across the table without handing it to the other side.
