Chapter 1

Sound is a wobble in pressure

The drone of an engine reaches your ear as the air's pressure rising and falling slightly, many times a second. Your eardrum is pushed in and pulled out in time with it.

Low sounds, like an engine's rumble, wobble slowly. High sounds, like a hiss, wobble quickly.

Try it. Watch the pressure at your ear rise and fall.

Chapter 2

Adding the opposite

A small microphone on the headphone hears the noise. The headphone plays the same wave turned upside down: where the noise pushes, the speaker pulls. The two pressures add, and if they match exactly, the sum is no wobble at all.

This is . The sound isn't absorbed or blocked; it is cancelled by more sound.

Try it. Switch the cancelling on and watch the sum flatten.

Chapter 3

Why low notes cancel best

The upside-down wave always arrives a moment late: the microphone has to hear, the electronics work out the reply, and the speaker has to move. Here that takes 50 millionths of a second.

For a slow 100 Hz hum, that is a tiny slice of one wobble, and the noise is cut to about 3% of its strength. At 1,000 Hz it is cut only to about a third. Above about 3,300 Hz the late wave starts to add to the noise instead. So headphones only cancel low sounds actively.

Try it. Raise the pitch and watch the cancelling fail.

Chapter 4

The cushions do the rest

High sounds are short waves, and short waves are easily stopped by a snug ear cushion or earbud tip. Low sounds are long waves that pass through almost anything, which is why you hear a neighbour's bass and not their voice.

So each half covers the other's gap: the electronics take out the rumble, and the seal takes out the chatter and hiss. Voices, in the middle, get through the most.

Try it. Switch each half on and off and see what is left.

Sources and notes

The cancelling in chapter 3 is worked out in your browser: a tone cancelled by its upside-down copy arriving τ seconds late is left with 2 × sin(π × frequency × τ) of its strength. 50 microseconds is a round figure chosen for illustration; real headphones also shape their reply by frequency. The cushion's blocking in chapter 4 is a smooth guess at a typical shape, not a measurement.
