Chapter 1

Light knocks electrons loose

A solar cell is a thin slice of silicon made in two layers: the top one treated to have spare electrons, the bottom one treated to be short of them. Where they meet, an electric field builds up across the join.

When light strikes the silicon, it can knock an electron free. The field at the join pushes freed electrons one way only, towards the top. Connect the two layers with a wire and they flow round it, back into the bottom layer: a current, made from light, with no moving parts.

Try it. Turn the sunlight up and down and watch the lamp.

Chapter 2

Facing the Sun

A panel catches the most light when it faces the Sun square on. Turn it away and it presents a narrower target to the rays: at 60° it catches only half as much.

That's why panels in Britain are tilted towards the south, roughly at the angle of the Sun in spring and autumn. Some large solar farms turn their panels to follow the Sun through the day.

Try it. Turn the panel and watch the band of light it catches.

Chapter 3

A day of sunshine

Through the day the Sun climbs and sinks, so the light on a panel rises to a peak at noon and falls away. Low in the sky, the light also crosses more air and arrives weaker.

In London, on a clear day, a panel tilted 35° to the south gets about three and a half times as much direct sunlight in June as in December: longer days, a higher Sun and a thinner layer of air. Clouds cut all of these figures, often by a great deal.

Try it. Compare a clear day in June, March and December.

Chapter 4

One fifth

Panels are rated in full sunshine of 1,000 watts on each square metre. A typical silicon panel turns about a fifth of that into electricity. The rest is reflected or warms the panel, and a hot panel works a little less well.

Some light lacks the energy to free an electron at all, and some has more than enough, the excess lost as heat. For a single layer of silicon that limits a cell to about a third, however well it is made.
