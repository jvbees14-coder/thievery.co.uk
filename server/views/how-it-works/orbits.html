Chapter 1

Newton's cannon

Isaac Newton imagined a cannon on a mountain so high it stood above the air. Fire a ball sideways and it curves down to the ground. Fire it faster and it lands further away.

But the Earth is round, so the ground curves away beneath a fast ball. Fire it fast enough and the ground falls away as fast as the ball falls towards it. The ball never lands. From 300 km up, that takes about 7.7 km a second.

Try it. Raise the speed until the ball goes all the way round.

Chapter 2

Always falling

An orbit isn't a place without gravity. At the space station's height, 400 km up, gravity is still about nine tenths as strong as on the ground. The station is falling the whole time; it is just moving sideways so fast, about 7.7 km a second, that it keeps missing.

Everything on board falls together, which is why astronauts float. It goes round the Earth about every 92 minutes, so its crew see sixteen sunrises a day.

Chapter 3

Higher is slower

Further out, gravity is weaker, so a circular orbit needs less speed, and the path round is longer. Both make a higher orbit take longer.

At 35,786 km up, one trip round takes as long as the Earth takes to turn once. A satellite there, over the equator, stays above the same spot on the ground. That is why satellite dishes for television point at one fixed place in the sky.

Try it. Change the height and watch the speed and the time round.

Chapter 4

Escape

Fire the ball faster than a circular orbit needs and it climbs higher on the far side, in a stretched oval. Faster still and the oval gets longer and longer.

At about 11 km a second from this height, the oval never closes: the ball slows as it climbs but never stops, and leaves the Earth for good. That is escape speed. Rockets to the Moon and planets have to reach it.

Try it. Push the speed past 11 km a second.
