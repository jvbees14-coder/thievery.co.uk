Chapter 1

Pressure goes everywhere

Fill two cylinders and the pipe between them with oil, and fit each with a piston. Push one piston down and the other rises. Oil barely squashes at all, so it passes the push along.

What it passes along is pressure: the push spread over the piston's area. Blaise Pascal noticed in the 1600s that pressure on a trapped liquid reaches every part of it equally. With two pistons the same size, what goes in comes out.

Try it. Push harder and watch the other side.

Chapter 2

A small push lifts a car

Now make the second piston bigger. The pressure is the same on both, but the big piston has more area for it to act on. If its area is fifty times the small one's, it pushes up fifty times as hard.

So a push like lifting 30 kg on the small piston lifts a 1,500 kg car on the big one. That is how a garage lift and a trolley jack work.

Try it. Find a push and a size of piston that lift the car.

Chapter 3

The price is distance

The oil pushed out of the small cylinder has to fill the big one. With fifty times the area, the big piston rises only a fiftieth as far: push the small one down a metre, and the car goes up 2 cm.

It's the same bargain as a lever or a pulley. Force times distance comes out equal at both ends, which is why a jack takes many pumps to lift a car a little way.

Try it. Push the small piston all the way down.

Chapter 4

One pedal, four wheels

A car's brakes are hydraulic. The pedal pushes a small piston; pipes of brake fluid carry the pressure to bigger pistons at every wheel, which squeeze pads against the discs. One foot stops a tonne and a half of car, with the pressure equal at all four wheels.

Diggers, cranes, aircraft controls and lorry tippers all work the same way: a pump makes pressure, and pistons of the right size turn it into the force needed.

Try it. Press the pedal.
