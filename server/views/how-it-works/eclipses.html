Chapter 1

Three in a line

An eclipse needs the Sun, Earth and Moon in a straight line. When the Moon is between the Sun and Earth, at new moon, its shadow can fall on Earth: a solar eclipse. When Earth is between, at full moon, Earth's shadow can fall on the Moon: a lunar eclipse.

There is a new moon and a full moon every month. Yet most months have no eclipse at all.

Try it. Move the Moon round its orbit and find the two lined-up points.

Chapter 2

A tilted orbit

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the path Earth follows round the Sun. That sounds small, but at the Moon's distance it puts the Moon up to about 34,000 km above or below the line, while Earth is under 13,000 km across.

So at most new moons the Moon's shadow passes above or below Earth, and at most full moons the Moon passes above or below Earth's shadow. Eclipses happen only when the line-up comes near one of the two points where the tilted orbit crosses the flat one, the .

Chapter 3

Eclipse seasons

The nodes point in a nearly fixed direction in space as Earth goes round the Sun. Twice a year, about 173 days apart, that direction lines up with the Sun, and for a month or so any new or full moon makes an eclipse. These are eclipse seasons.

Because each season lasts longer than a month, there is at least one solar eclipse in each, so between two and five every year. The nodes also creep round, one turn every 18.6 years, which is why the seasons fall a little earlier each year.

Try it. Run through a year and see which new moons make eclipses.

Chapter 4

Total, or a ring of fire

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times further away, so the two look almost exactly the same size. But the Moon's orbit is oval: near Earth it looks a little bigger than the Sun and covers it completely; far away it looks smaller and leaves a bright ring, an annular eclipse.

Even in a total eclipse the Moon's full shadow is a small spot, at most about 270 km across, sweeping over Earth. Any one place sees a total eclipse only once every few hundred years on average.

Try it. Change the Moon's distance and compare the two discs.
