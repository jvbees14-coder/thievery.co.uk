Chapter 1

A leaning planet

The Earth spins on an axis that leans 23.4° from upright. As it goes round the Sun, the axis keeps pointing the same way in space, towards the Pole Star.

So for half the year the northern half of the planet leans towards the Sun, and for the other half it leans away. In June the north leans in and has summer; in December the south does. In March and September neither half leans in, and day and night are about equal everywhere.

Try it. Move through the year, or press Play, and watch the axis keep its direction.

Chapter 2

High sun, low sun

Leaning in puts the Sun higher in the sky. In London the noon Sun climbs to 62° in late June but only 15° in late December.

A low Sun is a weak one. The same beam of sunlight lands on more ground when it comes in at a slant, so each square metre gets less. At the December noon it is spread over 3.4 times as much ground as in June.

Try it. Move the date and watch the patch of sunlight stretch.

Chapter 3

Long days, short days

The lean also changes how long the Sun is up. In London a June day has over 16 hours of daylight and a December day under 8. More hours of sunshine, each one stronger: that is summer.

The farther from the equator, the bigger the swing. At the equator every day is about 12 hours. North of the Arctic Circle, at 66.6°, the Sun doesn't set at midsummer and doesn't rise at midwinter.

Try it. Change the latitude and watch the curve stretch.

Chapter 4

It isn't the distance

A common guess is that summer comes when the Earth is nearest the Sun. The orbit is an oval, but a barely oval one: the Earth is closest, 147.1 million km, in early January, and farthest, 152.1 million km, in early July.

So the north has its winter when the Earth is closest to the Sun. If distance were what mattered, the whole planet would have summer at once. Instead, the two hemispheres take turns.

Try it. This is the orbit drawn to scale. Move through the year and see how little the distance changes.
