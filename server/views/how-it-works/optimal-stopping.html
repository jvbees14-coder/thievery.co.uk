Chapter 1

One chance at each

Ten flats to view, one after another, in a random order. After each viewing you must take it or turn it down for good; somebody else will have it by tomorrow. You want the best of the ten.

Take the first and you get the best one time in ten. Wait for the last and the same. A better plan is to look without taking for a while, then take the first one better than everything you have seen.

Try it. Set how many to look at first and run the viewings.

Chapter 2

How long to look

Look at too few and you take something early, before you know what good looks like. Look at too many and the best has probably gone by.

With ten flats, looking at three and then taking the first that beats them finds the very best 39.9% of the time. Looking at four is almost as good, at 39.8%.

Try it. Run a thousand searches for each choice and compare them with the exact chances.

Chapter 3

The 37% rule

As the number of choices grows, the best plan settles down: look at the first 37% without taking any, then take the first one better than all of those. It finds the best about 37% of the time, however many there are.

Both figures are 1 divided by . That the chance stays at 37% even among a million choices is the surprise: the long wait does not make it hopeless.

Try it. Raise the number of choices and watch the best plan settle.

Chapter 4

The cost of the rule

The rule still misses the best 63% of the time. Often it takes a good one that isn't the best. But when the best was among those you only looked at, nothing later beats it, and you are left with whatever comes last: that happens as often as the share you looked at, 37%.

Looking at fewer makes that unlikely, at a small cost to finding the very best. Real choices are rarely all or nothing, which is why people usually stop sooner.

Try it. Move the cut-off and see how the outcomes change.

Sources and notes

The exact chances are worked out in your browser: looking at r of n and then taking the first better one finds the best with chance (r/n) × (1/r + 1/(r+1) + … + 1/(n−1)). The runs are simulated with random orders. This is the "secretary problem", studied since the 1950s.
