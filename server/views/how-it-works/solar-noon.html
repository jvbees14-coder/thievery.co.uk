Chapter 1

The sundial and the clock

Noon ought to be when the Sun is highest, due south. But put a sundial beside a clock and they rarely agree, even at Greenwich. In early November the sundial runs over 16 minutes ahead; in mid-February it's over 14 minutes behind.

Only four times a year, around mid-April, mid-June, the start of September and Christmas, do they match. The gap is called the equation of time.

Chapter 2

The tilt's share

Clocks assume the Sun moves round the sky at an even pace, once every 24 hours. The Sun moves steadily along its own path, but that path is tilted 23.4° to the equator. Near the equinoxes much of its motion is up or down rather than east, and near the solstices it is all east and spread over shorter circles.

So measured along the equator, which is what clocks count, the Sun runs fast and slow twice a year, by up to about 10 minutes.

Chapter 3

The orbit's share

The Earth's orbit is slightly oval, and the Earth moves faster when it is nearer the Sun, in early January. Moving faster along its orbit, it has to turn a little further each day to bring the Sun back to due south, so solar days run slightly long in winter.

This adds a slower swing, once a year, of up to about 8 minutes. Added to the tilt's part, it makes the lopsided curve of the first chapter.

Try it. The coloured line is the orbit's part alone.

Chapter 4

A figure of eight

Photograph the Sun at exactly 12:00 by the clock every few days for a year and it doesn't stay in one place. It climbs and falls with the seasons, and drifts east and west with the equation of time, tracing a figure of eight called an analemma.

Before railways, every town kept its own sundial time. Railway timetables needed one time for everyone, and in 1847 Britain's railways adopted Greenwich time, an average Sun that keeps perfect step and never quite matches the real one.

Try it. Follow the Sun round its figure of eight through the year.
