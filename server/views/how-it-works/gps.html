How it works

How GPS finds you

Your phone works out where it is from satellites it never speaks to. Here is how, one piece at a time.

{{hiw-stage}}

Chapter 1

The big picture

There are 32 GPS satellites in service. Each one is about 20,200 km up, moving at 3.87 km/s, and goes round the Earth once every 11 hours 58 minutes. They fly in six rings tipped 55° to the equator, so that wherever you stand, around ten of them are above your horizon right now.

The picture is to scale. The Earth really is that small next to the orbits. We have put you in London.

Your phone never sends the satellites anything. It only listens, and the rest of this page is how listening is enough.

Chapter 2

Satellites shout the time

Each satellite carries and broadcasts the same two things, over and over: the exact time by its clock, and exactly where it is (its ).

The rings are those broadcasts spreading out at the speed of light. Here they are slowed down 20 times. At full speed, one reaches you in about a fifteenth of a second.

The labels are what four of the satellites in view are saying: who they are, and when they sent it, to the millionth of a second.

Chapter 3

Delay is distance

Your phone notes when each message arrives and compares that with the time stamped on it. The difference is how long the signal was travelling.

Light covers 299,792 km every second, so a travel time is a distance: multiply one by the other. A satellite straight overhead is about 20,200 km away and its signal takes 67 ms. One on the horizon is nearer 25,800 km away, and its signal takes 86 ms.

This view is a flat slice through the Earth, with one satellite going over.

Try it. Drag the satellite across the sky and watch the travel time change. Drag it below the horizon and the signal stops: GPS needs a clear line to the sky.

Chapter 4

Where the circles meet

One distance says how far you are from a satellite, not where. In space, that puts you somewhere on a sphere around it.

In this flat slice every sphere becomes a circle. One satellite gives you a circle. Two give the two places the circles cross. A third leaves one. Finding a place from distances like this is called .

In three dimensions it takes one more step: one satellite gives a sphere, two a circle, three just two points. One of those is usually far out in space, so the receiver can throw it away.

How the circles cross matters as much as how many there are. Satellites bunched in one part of the sky cross at shallow angles, and a small error in one range slides the crossing a long way. That is what "Fix good to" measures, assuming each range is good to 5 m. It is called .

Try it. Switch satellites off to see the fix fail. Drag two of them close together and watch the fix get worse.

Chapter 5

The clock problem

All of this assumes your phone knows the time to a millionth of a second. It doesn't. A phone keeps time with a , not an atomic clock, and it can easily be out by far more than that.

Light travels about 300 m in a microsecond (μs). So a phone clock that is out by 1 μs puts every range out by 300 m. The same clock times every signal, so every range is out by the same amount, and the circles stop meeting.

The answer is to treat the clock error as one more unknown and solve for it. In this slice two satellites fix a position, so a third pins down the clock. In three dimensions it takes three for the position, so a fourth satellite pins down the clock. That is why a receiver needs at least four, and why your phone ends up knowing the time better than it did.

Try it. Move the clock error and watch the gap open up. Then switch on "Solve for the clock error".

Chapter 6

Einstein matters

The satellites' clocks have a problem of their own: in orbit, they don't tick at the same rate as clocks on the ground.

They move at 3.87 km/s, and a moving clock runs slow (): about 7.2 μs a day. They are also 20,200 km further from the Earth's mass, where gravity is weaker and clocks run fast (): about 45.7 μs a day. Together, a satellite clock gains about 38 μs a day.

Light travels about 11.5 km in 38 μs. Left alone, every range would drift by that much more each day.

So the clocks are set to run slow before launch: 10.22999999543 MHz rather than 10.23 MHz, which in orbit comes out exactly right. The receiver corrects the smaller effects that are left.

Because every satellite clock gains at almost the same rate, a receiver solving for its own clock error would soak up much of the drift. But GPS is also the clock that phone networks, power grids and banks set their time by, and a time signal 38 μs out after one day would be no use to any of them.

Try it. Play the day with the corrections off. Then switch off gravity: speed alone makes the satellite clocks fall behind.
