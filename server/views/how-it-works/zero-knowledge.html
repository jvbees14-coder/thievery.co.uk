Chapter 1

A cave with a locked door

Peggy says she knows the secret word that opens a door. Victor wants proof, but Peggy won't say the word, or he'd know it too. Can she convince him without giving anything away?

Picture a cave shaped like a ring, entered from one point, with the locked door on the far side. The two ways round meet at the door, so only someone with the word can get from one side to the other.

Chapter 2

Come out on the left

Victor waits outside while Peggy walks in, by the left or the right; he can't see which. Then he shouts which side he wants her to come out of. If she knows the word she can always do it, passing through the door when she has to.

Victor learns nothing about the word: all he ever sees is Peggy coming out where he asked. He couldn't even convince anyone else, since a film of it could have been staged.

Try it. Play a few rounds. Peggy never fails.

Chapter 3

A bluffer gets caught

Now suppose Peggy doesn't know the word. She has to guess which side Victor will call before she goes in. Half the time she guesses right and comes out where he asked. The other half she is stuck on the wrong side of the door.

One round proves little. But every round is a fresh coin toss for a bluffer, and one wrong call exposes her.

Try it. See how many rounds the bluffer survives.

Chapter 4

Twenty rounds

Each round halves a bluffer's chance of getting through. After 20 rounds, it's about one in a million. Victor never learns the word, yet ends up as sure as he likes that Peggy knows it.

Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff described zero-knowledge proofs in 1985, and the cave story came in 1989. Today the same idea, with mathematics in place of caves, lets people prove things such as being old enough, or owning a key, without revealing anything more.
