Chapter 1

You can't flatten an orange

Try to flatten an orange peel and it tears or wrinkles. The surface of a sphere simply cannot be laid flat without stretching some of it. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved it in 1827: no flat map of the world can get every distance right.

So every world map is a compromise, called a . Each one decides what to keep true (shapes, areas, directions, distances along some lines) and what to give up.

Chapter 2

Mercator

Gerardus Mercator drew his map in 1569 for sailors. On it, a line at a constant compass bearing is a straight line, so a navigator could rule a course and steer it. To make that work, the map keeps angles true everywhere, and stretches things more and more towards the poles to do it.

At 60° north, everything is drawn twice as wide and twice as tall as at the equator, so four times the area. By Greenland's latitude it is about ten times.

Try it. Pick up Greenland and drag it down towards the equator. It is the same piece of land the whole way; only the map changes its size.

Chapter 3

Circles that grow

In 1859 Nicolas Tissot found a neat way to see what a projection does. Draw identical small circles all over the globe, then draw them through the map.

On Mercator they stay circles, because angles are kept, but they swell towards the poles. On the equirectangular map, a plain grid of latitude and longitude, they stretch sideways. On an equal-area map they all keep the same area, and get squashed instead.

Try it. Switch between the projections and watch the circles.

Chapter 4

Equal area

Equal-area projections keep every country's size in proportion, at the cost of bending shapes near the edges. On one, Africa is plainly about fourteen times the size of Greenland, which is the truth.

The Mollweide projection, from 1805, is an ellipse. Equal Earth, designed in 2018, is gentler on shapes and is used by NASA and others for world maps.

Try it. Send Greenland to the equator on Mercator, then switch to Equal Earth and see it shrink to its real size.

Chapter 5

Every map is a choice

None of these is wrong. Mercator is still the right map for navigating, and web maps use a version of it because it keeps streets at right angles when you zoom in. An equal-area map is the right one for comparing countries. Nothing is right for everything.

The numbers compare Greenland with Australia as drawn: the area of the shape on screen. Greenland really has a little over a quarter of Australia's area.

Try it. Move Greenland anywhere and flick between the four projections.
