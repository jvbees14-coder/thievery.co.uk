Chapter 1

Blink one eye, then the other

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps sideways against the wall behind it. Bring it closer and it jumps further. This shift is .

Your eyes are about 6.5 cm apart. Knowing that and the angle of the jump, the finger's distance follows from a thin triangle, which is how your brain judges depth.

Try it. Switch eyes and move the finger closer.

Chapter 2

Eyes 300 million km apart

Stars are so far away that two eyes see no shift at all. But Earth moves: in January it is on one side of the Sun and in July on the other, about 300 million km apart. A nearby star seen six months apart shifts against much more distant ones behind it.

Over a year it traces a tiny loop and returns. The nearer the star, the bigger the loop.

Try it. Run the year and watch the near star shift against the far ones.

Chapter 3

The parsec

Astronomers measure the shift as half the full swing, in . A star at the distance where that is exactly one arcsecond is one parsec away, from "parallax second": about 3.26 light years.

The rule is then simple: the distance in parsecs is one divided by the shift in arcseconds. Half an arcsecond is 2 parsecs; a tenth is 10.

Try it. Move the star further away and read off its shift.

Chapter 4

How small the angles are

Even the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, shifts by only 0.77 arcseconds: the width of a 2p coin seen from about 7 km away. That is why the first measurement took until 1838, when Friedrich Bessel caught 61 Cygni shifting by about 0.3 arcseconds.

Above the blurring air, the Gaia spacecraft has since measured the shifts of almost 1.5 billion stars, and with them the shape of our part of the galaxy.

Try it. Pick a star and see how far away the coin would be.

Sources and notes
