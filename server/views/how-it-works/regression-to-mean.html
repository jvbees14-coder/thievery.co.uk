Chapter 1

Skill plus luck

Any score is partly skill and partly luck: a good night's sleep, a lucky guess, a gust of wind. Give four hundred people the same test twice and plot each person's first score against their second.

If only skill counted, everybody would score the same both times and the dots would sit on the diagonal line. The more luck counts, the more the cloud spreads out, and the less the first score tells you about the second.

Try it. Turn the luck up and down and watch the cloud.

Chapter 2

The best do worse

Pick out the top tenth from the first test and look at their second scores. On average they fall back towards the middle. Here, with luck counting as much as skill, the top tenth lose about half their lead.

Nothing happened to them. To reach the top tenth you usually needed some skill and some good luck too, and the good luck doesn't come back the second time. This is regression to the mean, and Francis Galton first described it in the 1880s, studying the heights of parents and children.

Try it. Make new people a few times. The drop is there every time.

Chapter 3

Praise and blame

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman told of flight instructors who were sure that praise made trainees worse and shouting made them better. After a smooth landing they praised, and the next landing was usually worse. After a rough one they shouted, and the next was usually better.

Here is a pilot whose skill never changes; every landing is their usual standard plus luck. The landings after the best ones are still worse, and the ones after the worst are still better, with no praise or shouting at all.

Try it. Compare the praised landings with the ones that came next.

Chapter 4

The worst do better

It works the other way too: whoever did worst the first time does better the next, on average, whatever is done for them. That makes it easy to fool yourself.

A road gets a speed camera after a bad year for crashes, and next year is better. A struggling team sacks its manager and recovers. Some of that improvement would have come anyway. To know whether the change worked, compare with places or people who were picked the same way but left alone.

Try it. Look at the bottom tenth's second scores.
