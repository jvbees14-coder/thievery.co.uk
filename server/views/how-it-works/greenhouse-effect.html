Chapter 1

Heat in, heat out

The Earth is warmed by sunlight and cools by giving off heat into space, as infrared light we can't see. The hotter something is, the more infrared it gives off. So the planet settles at the temperature where it loses heat exactly as fast as sunlight brings it.

Averaged over the whole globe, day and night, sunlight brings about 340 watts to each square metre. About 30% bounces straight back off clouds, ice and land, leaving 238 to be absorbed.

Chapter 2

A planet without air

Work out the temperature at which the ground would give off 238 watts per square metre, and you get about −18 °C. That is what the Earth's average would be with nothing between the ground and space.

The real average is about 15 °C, 33 degrees warmer. The difference is the air.

Try it. Make the planet more or less reflective. A whiter planet is colder.

Chapter 3

A layer that sends heat back

Nitrogen and oxygen let infrared pass. But water vapour, carbon dioxide and a few other gases absorb it, and give it off again in every direction, including back down. Sunlight passes through them mostly untouched; the infrared on its way out doesn't.

With some of its heat sent back, the ground has to warm until enough still escapes to balance the sunlight. In this simple model, air that absorbs 78% of the ground's infrared brings the average to about 15 °C, matching the real one. This is the greenhouse effect, and without it the Earth would be frozen.

Try it. Change how much infrared the air absorbs and watch the ground's temperature.

Chapter 4

Adding carbon dioxide

Before industry there were about 280 parts per million of carbon dioxide in the air; burning coal, oil and gas has raised it to about 420. More of it absorbs more of the outgoing infrared, so the balance shifts warmer.

Each doubling adds roughly the same warming once the climate has settled. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's best estimate is 3 °C per doubling, likely between 2.5 and 4 °C. The world has warmed by about 1.1 °C so far: the oceans take decades to catch up, and haze from pollution has hidden some of it.

Try it. Move the CO₂ level and read off the eventual warming.

Sources and notes

Chapters 1 to 3 use the textbook one-layer model: real air has many layers, clouds, and gases that absorb some wavelengths and not others, so the model's arrows are not the measured ones, though its temperatures match. Chapter 4's curve is the warming once things settle, worked out from the logarithm of the CO₂ level.
