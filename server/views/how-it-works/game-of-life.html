Chapter 1

Three rules

Take a grid of squares, each alive or dead. Every tick of the clock, each square counts its eight neighbours. A live square with two or three live neighbours survives; with fewer it dies of loneliness, with more of overcrowding. A dead square with exactly three live neighbours comes to life.

That's all. The mathematician John Conway devised it in 1970. Some shapes sit still for ever, like the square block. Others flip back and forth, like the line of three called a blinker.

Try it. Step a generation at a time and check the rules for yourself.

Chapter 2

Something that moves

Five live squares in the right shape do something nobody programmed: they walk. Every four generations the pattern reappears one square further along, diagonally. It's called a glider.

Nothing in the rules mentions movement. The glider is behaviour that emerges from simple local rules, which is the whole fascination of the game.

Try it. Watch the glider cross the grid and come back round the edge.

Chapter 3

Order from a random start

Scatter live squares at random and let it run. At first it boils; then it settles into a scattering of still blocks, blinking lines and the odd glider wandering off.

Whether a pattern will die, settle or grow for ever can't, in general, be worked out without running it. Life turns out to be able to compute anything a computer can, given a large enough grid.

Try it. Start a few random soups and see how they settle.

Chapter 4

A glider gun

Conway wondered whether any pattern could grow without limit, and offered a prize. Bill Gosper won it in 1970 with this: a pattern of 36 cells that shuttles back and forth and fires off a new glider every 30 generations, for ever.

Streams of gliders can carry signals and collide like logic gates. People have since built working clocks, calculators and even a version of Life itself, all inside Life.

Try it. Watch the gun fire and count the gliders.
