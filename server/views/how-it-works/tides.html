Chapter 1

The Moon's pull

The Moon is 384,400 km away, and its gravity reaches all of the Earth. But gravity weakens with distance, so it doesn't pull every part of the Earth equally.

The side of the Earth facing the Moon is about 12,700 km nearer than the far side. It is pulled a little harder than the Earth's centre, and the far side a little less.

The picture looks down on the North Pole. The Moon is drawn much closer than it really is, so the differences are big enough to see.

Chapter 2

Two bulges, not one

The whole Earth is falling towards the Moon at the rate its centre is pulled. Take that shared pull away from every arrow and what is left is the difference, called the .

It stretches the Earth along the line to the Moon: outwards on the near side, where the water is pulled ahead of the Earth, and outwards on the far side, where the Earth is pulled away from the water. Round the middle, it squeezes inwards.

So the oceans pile into two bulges, one under the Moon and one opposite it. If the Earth were all ocean, each would be only about half a metre high.

Chapter 3

Twice a day

The Earth turns inside the bulges, so any coast is carried through both of them each day: two high tides, two low.

Not quite twice in 24 hours, though. While the Earth turns, the Moon moves on round its orbit, so it takes 24 hours and 50 minutes for a place to come back under it. High tides arrive about 12 hours 25 minutes apart, and about 50 minutes later each day.

Try it. Drag the hour slider and watch London pass through a bulge on the graph.

Chapter 4

The Sun joins in

The Sun raises bulges too. It is vastly heavier than the Moon but about 390 times further away, and the tidal force falls with the cube of distance, so its tides are only about 46% as strong as the Moon's.

At new Moon and full Moon, the Sun and the Moon line up and their bulges add: the biggest tides, called spring tides. At the quarter Moons they pull at right angles and partly cancel: the smallest, neap tides. The cycle repeats every two weeks.

Try it. Move through the month and watch the bulges grow and shrink with the Moon's phase.

Chapter 5

Real coasts

Half a metre is not what you see on a British beach. Real tides are the bulges sloshing round ocean basins, which have shapes and sizes of their own.

Where a bay or an estuary narrows like a funnel, and the water in it swings back and forth in time with the tides, the range grows enormously. The Severn Estuary, between Wales and England, has one of the largest in the world, around 14 m. Canada's Bay of Fundy reaches about 16 m.

Elsewhere the swings cancel out, and some coasts see very little tide at all.
