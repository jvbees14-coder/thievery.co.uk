Chapter 1

Friends of friends

Picture 200 people standing in a circle, each friends with the three nearest on either side. It's a cosy world: most of your friends know each other, and a message passed friend to friend creeps round a few places at a time.

To reach someone on the far side takes over 30 steps, and the average is about 17.

Chapter 2

A few long links

Now swap a handful of friendships for ones with anyone at all: a cousin abroad, a friend from university. Just one friendship in a hundred is enough to halve the average distance between people.

Each long link is a shortcut for everyone near either end of it. Meanwhile your own circle hardly changes; most of your friends still know each other.

Try it. Rewire a few per cent and watch the average steps fall.

Chapter 3

Small worlds

In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz plotted both measures as more friendships were rewired. Path length falls almost at once; the closeness of friendship circles stays high until far more are rewired.

In between lies a "small world": close-knit groups, yet only a few steps from anyone. They found the same pattern in film actors working together, in the US power grid, and in the nerves of a worm.

Try it. Find the stretch where the red line is low but the blue line is still high.

Chapter 4

Six degrees

In 1967 Stanley Milgram asked people in Nebraska and Kansas to get a letter to a stranger in Boston, passing it only to someone they knew personally. The letters that arrived took about five intermediate steps on average: the origin of "six degrees of separation".

Online networks are smaller still. In 2016 Facebook's researchers found that, on average, any two of its users were linked by about three and a half steps.

Try it. Pick two people on opposite sides and see the chain between them.
