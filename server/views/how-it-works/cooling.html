Chapter 1

A cooling cup

A cup of coffee cools quickly at first and then more and more slowly, creeping towards the temperature of the room but never quite reaching it.

Isaac Newton described this in 1701: an object loses heat at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than its surroundings. The result is a curve that falls by the same fraction of the remaining gap every minute.

Try it. Change the room's temperature and see how long the coffee takes to be drinkable.

Chapter 2

Hot things cool faster

Straight from the pot, 70 degrees hotter than the room, this cup loses about 4 degrees a minute. Twenty minutes later, with the gap down to about 23 degrees, it loses just over 1 a minute.

That one fact settles an old argument.

Try it. Compare the steepness of the curve at each point.

Chapter 3

Milk now or later?

You won't drink your coffee for ten minutes and want it as hot as possible. Should you add the cold milk now, or just before drinking?

Now. Adding milk straight away cools the coffee at once, so for the next ten minutes it's nearer the room's temperature and loses heat more slowly. Left black, it stays hotter and so cools faster, then the milk knocks it down at the end anyway. Here the difference is about a degree after ten minutes, growing with a longer wait.

Try it. Change how long you wait and compare the two cups.

Chapter 4

Slowing it down

How fast a cup cools also depends on how easily heat escapes: through the cup's sides, from the open top as steam, and as radiation. A thin cup cools faster than a thick mug; a lid stops most of the steam; a vacuum flask, with nothing between its walls to carry heat, keeps coffee hot for hours.

Each changes only the rate in Newton's rule, not its shape: the curve still falls by a steady fraction of the gap, just more slowly.

Try it. Swap the cup and see how long the coffee stays hot.
