Chapter 1

One die is flat

Roll a fair die and every face is as likely as any other: one chance in six each. Roll it a few thousand times and count, and the six bars come out about level.

Nothing about one die is bell-shaped. The bell comes from adding.

Try it. Let the rolls pile up and watch the bars even out.

Chapter 2

Two dice make a peak

Add two dice and the totals are no longer equal. There is only one way to make 2 (a one and a one) but six ways to make 7: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4, and each of those the other way round. So 7 comes up 6 times in 36, about 17% of rolls, and 2 just once in 36.

Middling totals win because there are more ways to reach them.

Try it. Count the ways to each total, then roll and compare.

Chapter 3

More dice, a smoother bell

With three dice the triangle rounds off. With ten, the totals run from 10 to 60, but 95% of rolls land between 25 and 45, and the most likely total, 35, comes up only about 7% of the time. The shape is the .

The bell is wider in total as dice are added, but narrower compared with the whole range, because high and low rolls cancel each other out more often than they line up.

Try it. Add dice one at a time and watch the bell form.

Chapter 4

Even with a crooked die

Here is a badly loaded die: it shows a one half the time, a two, three or four otherwise, and never a five or six. Its single-roll bars are nothing like a bell.

Add twenty of them and the bell is back, centred on 40. This is the : add up enough independent bits of chance and the total is bell-shaped, whatever each bit looks like. It is why heights, measurement errors and the average of a poll all come out close to a bell.

Try it. Switch between the fair and the loaded die and add dice.
