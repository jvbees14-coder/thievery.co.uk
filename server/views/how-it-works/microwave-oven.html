Chapter 1

Twisting water

A microwave oven fills its metal box with radio waves at 2.45 billion cycles a second, each wave about 12 cm long. Their electric field flips back and forth that many times a second.

A water molecule is lopsided, slightly positive at one end and negative at the other, so it twists to line up with the field, and twists back as the field flips. All that twisting jostles neighbouring molecules, and jostling is heat. It isn't a special resonance of water; any food with water in it warms this way.

Try it. Watch the molecule follow the wave.

Chapter 2

Hot spots

Inside the box, the waves bounce off the metal walls and overlap. Where crests meet crests they add up; where crests meet troughs they cancel. The result is a standing pattern that doesn't move: strong spots and dead spots, about half a wavelength, 6 cm, apart.

Food left still cooks in a pattern of hot and cold patches. That's why a microwaved meal can be scalding in one place and frozen an inch away.

Try it. Cook a plate that doesn't turn, and look at the patches.

Chapter 3

Why it turns

The turntable carries each bit of food through hot spots and cold spots in turn, so they even out. What's left are rings: the middle of the plate stays on one spot, which is why the centre of a dish often heats least.

Some ovens use a spinning metal stirrer above the food instead, which keeps moving the pattern itself. Stirring the food halfway through, as the packet says, does the same job.

Try it. Cook again on the turntable and compare.

Chapter 4

The speed of light from chocolate

Take the turntable out and heat a bar of chocolate briefly. It melts in spots along the bar, at the hot spots, about 6.1 cm apart: half a wavelength.

A wave's speed is its wavelength times its frequency. So twice the gap between the spots, times the 2.45 GHz printed on the back of the oven, gives about 300,000 km a second: the speed of light, which is what microwaves are.

Try it. Set the gap you measured and read off the speed.
