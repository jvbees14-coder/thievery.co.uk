Chapter 1

Into a raindrop and back

A rainbow is sunlight that has gone into raindrops and come back out towards you. Light bends as it enters water, bounces off the back of the drop, and bends again as it leaves.

Where the light comes out depends on where it went in. A ray through the middle comes straight back. One that strikes further out comes back at a wider angle to the sunlight, up to a point.

Try it. Move where the ray strikes the drop and watch the angle it comes back at.

Chapter 2

Forty-two degrees

However you aim the ray, it never comes back at more than about 42°. And close to that limit, many different rays come out at almost the same angle, so the light piles up there.

That pile-up is the rainbow. About a fifth of the light that comes back out of a drop leaves within one degree of 42°. The drop sends some light back at every smaller angle too, but spread thin.

Try it. Look at the bars: where the light comes out, and where it bunches.

Chapter 3

Splitting the colours

Water bends violet light a little more than red. So each colour has its own limit: about 42.4° for red and 40.7° for violet, with the rest in between.

That is why the colours come out in bands, always in the same order, with red on the outside of the bow and violet on the inside.

Try it. Move the ray and watch white light fan out into colours.

Chapter 4

A circle round your shadow

Every drop that sends you light at 42° from the point directly opposite the Sun lights up, and those drops lie on a circle round that point. The point is where the shadow of your head falls. So a rainbow is a circle, and the ground usually hides the lower part.

As the Sun climbs, the point opposite sinks, and the bow sinks with it. Once the Sun is higher than about 42°, the whole bow is below the horizon, which is why rainbows are a morning and evening sight. From an aeroplane you can sometimes see the whole circle.

Try it. Raise the Sun and watch the bow go down.

Chapter 5

The second bow

Some light bounces twice inside the drop before it leaves. It makes a second, fainter bow outside the first, at about 50° to 53°, with its colours the other way round: red on the inside.

Between the two the sky is darker, because no light from one or two bounces comes back at those angles. It is called Alexander's band, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it around AD 200.

Sources and notes

The rays are traced in your browser through a perfectly round drop, using water's refractive index for each colour. The angles in the text come out of that tracing, and agree with the published figures. In chapter 3 the spread between the colours is drawn five times wider than it is, so that it can be seen. Real raindrops are not quite round, and very small ones blur the colours.
