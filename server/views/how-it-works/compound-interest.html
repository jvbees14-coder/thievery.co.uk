Chapter 1

Interest on interest

Put £1,000 somewhere that pays 5% a year. If the interest is paid out and spent, you earn £50 every year: after 30 years, £1,500 of interest on top of your £1,000. That is simple interest.

Leave the interest in, and next year you earn 5% on £1,050, then on £1,102.50, and so on. Each year's interest earns interest of its own. After 30 years the £1,000 has become £4,321.94.

Try it. Stretch the years and watch the gap between the two lines open up.

Chapter 2

How often it's added

In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked a simple question. £1 earns 100% a year. If the interest is added twice a year at 50% each time, you end with £2.25, not £2. Monthly, £2.61. Daily, £2.71.

It keeps growing but never passes £2.71828…, a number now called e. It turns up everywhere growth feeds on itself, from bacteria to radioactive decay.

Try it. Compound more and more often, and watch it creep towards e.

Chapter 3

The rule of 72

A shortcut for how long anything growing at a steady rate takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly percentage. At 6%, about 12 years; at 9%, about 8.

It is a rough rule but a good one for ordinary rates, because 72 is close to the true figure and divides neatly by so many numbers.

Try it. Change the rate and compare the rule with the exact answer.

Chapter 4

A little every month

Paying in a small amount every month adds up the same way: every payment starts earning from the day it arrives. Over long stretches, most of the final total can be growth rather than money paid in.

That makes time the strongest part of the sum. Money paid in early has longest to grow, so starting later costs more than the payments that were missed.

Try it. Start ten years later and compare the totals.

Chapter 5

It works against you too

Compounding doesn't care whose side it is on. A debt left unpaid at a high rate grows the same way: £1,000 owed at 22% a year, with nothing paid off, is more than £7,000 after ten years.

Prices do it as well. At 2% inflation a year, what £1,000 buys today, about £500 will buy in 35 years. Savings growing more slowly than prices are shrinking.

This page is arithmetic, not advice. Real accounts have fees, taxes and changing rates.
