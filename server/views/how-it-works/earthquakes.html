Chapter 1

Two waves

When rock gives way deep underground, the shock spreads out as waves through the Earth. Two kinds travel through solid rock. P waves push and pull the rock along their direction of travel, like sound, and are fast: about 6 km a second in the crust. S waves shake it from side to side, and travel at about 3.5 km a second.

A seismometer far away feels the P wave first, as a small jolt, and the bigger S wave some time later.

Try it. Watch the waves spread and the trace record each one.

Chapter 2

The gap tells the distance

The farther the seismometer, the more the slow S wave falls behind. Every second of gap between the P and S arrivals means about 8.4 km of distance. A gap of 12 seconds puts the quake about 100 km away.

So a single seismometer can tell how far away a quake was, though not in which direction.

Try it. Move the seismometer and watch the gap grow.

Chapter 3

Three circles

Each station knows only its distance, so the quake could be anywhere on a circle round it. Two stations' circles cross in two places. A third station's circle picks out one of them.

Real networks use many stations, the exact arrival times and the way wave speeds change with depth, and can place a quake within a few kilometres in minutes. It is the same idea as GPS, run backwards.

Try it. Draw the circles one at a time, then make a new quake.

Chapter 4

Each step is a big step

Magnitude grows by one each time the shaking on a seismometer grows ten times. The energy grows faster still: each step releases about 32 times as much.

So a magnitude 7 releases about a thousand times the energy of a magnitude 5, and a magnitude 9, like the one off Japan in 2011, about a thousand times that of a magnitude 7. Big quakes are far rarer than small ones, but they carry nearly all the energy.

Try it. Compare a magnitude 6 and a magnitude 7 with magnitude 5s.
