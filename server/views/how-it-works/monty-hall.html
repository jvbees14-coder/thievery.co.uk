Chapter 1

Three doors

A prize is behind one of three doors; the other two hide nothing. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the prize is, then opens one of the other two, always an empty one, and offers you a choice: stick with your door, or switch to the last closed one.

Most people say it can't matter: two doors left, so it's fifty-fifty. It isn't. Switching wins two times in three.

Try it. Play a few games, sometimes sticking and sometimes switching.

Chapter 2

Thousands of games

A few games prove nothing, so here are thousands, played both ways. Sticking settles on a third. Switching settles on two thirds.

The reason: your first pick is right one time in three, and opening an empty door doesn't change that. In the other two games in three, the prize is behind one of the doors you didn't pick, and the host has just shown you which.

Try it. Press Play, or play a thousand at once.

Chapter 3

A hundred doors

If it still feels wrong, make the game bigger. A hundred doors, one prize. You pick one: a one-in-a-hundred chance. The host, who knows, opens ninety-eight empty doors and leaves one other shut.

Either you found the prize first time, or it's behind the one door the host carefully stepped around. Switching now wins ninety-nine times in a hundred.

Try it. Pick a door, and look at which one the host left shut.

Chapter 4

It's the host's knowledge

Everything depends on the host knowing. Suppose instead he opens one of the other doors at random, and it just happens to be empty. Now his choice tells you nothing, and sticking and switching really are equal.

The puzzle became famous in 1990, when Marilyn vos Savant answered it in Parade magazine: switch. Thousands of readers wrote in to say she was wrong, many of them with doctorates. She wasn't.

Try it. These games use a host who doesn't know (games where he opens the prize door are thrown away). Watch both bars settle on a half.
