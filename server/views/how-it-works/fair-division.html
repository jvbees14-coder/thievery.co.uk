Chapter 1

One cuts, the other chooses

Two people share a cake that is half chocolate and half vanilla. Ann loves chocolate, three times as much as vanilla; Ben likes both the same. The oldest fair method is the one children use: one cuts, the other chooses.

Ann cuts. Whatever she does, Ben will take the piece he prefers, so her safest cut is one where she wouldn't mind which piece she gets: a third of the way along, where both pieces are worth half the cake to her. Ben then takes the bigger piece, worth two thirds to him.

Try it. Be Ann and find the cut that does best for you.

Chapter 2

Nobody can complain

Each gets at least half the cake by their own reckoning, and neither would swap. Fair division calls this . It doesn't need anybody to agree what the cake is worth, or to know what the other likes.

Because tastes differ, both can do better than half. Swap the roles and Ben cuts it down the middle; Ann takes the chocolate side and has three quarters of the cake by her measure. When tastes differ, the chooser usually comes off better.

Try it. Be Ben and see what Ann does with your cut.

Chapter 3

Three people and a moving knife

Add Cy, who prefers vanilla. One way to share among three: slide a knife slowly along the cake, and the first person to think the piece so far is worth a third shouts "stop" and takes it. The other two carry on with what is left.

Every person ends up with at least a third, by their own measure. Here Ann shouts first, then Ben, and Cy takes the rest.

Try it. Move the knife along and see who shouts.

Chapter 4

A third is not enough

But a fair share is not the same as no envy. Ann's third is worth 33% to her, while she reckons Ben's piece is worth 44%. Ben feels the same about Cy's.

Envy-free methods for three people exist, the first found by John Selfridge around 1960 and again by John Conway in 1993, with a few extra cuts and trims. For any number of people, a method that always finishes was only found in 2016, and the number of cuts it can need is astronomical.

Try it. Read across the table to see who envies whom.
