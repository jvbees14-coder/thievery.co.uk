Chapter 1

Four strokes

A petrol engine burns a mixture of air and fuel in a closed cylinder and lets the hot gas push a piston. It does it in four steps, each one a single stroke of the piston, up or down.

Intake: the piston moves down with the intake valve open, drawing in air and fuel. Compression: both valves shut and the piston moves up, squeezing the mixture. Power: a spark lights it, and the burning gas drives the piston down. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens and the piston pushes the burnt gas out.

Only one stroke in four does any work. The others are paid for by the momentum of the crankshaft and by the other cylinders.

Try it. Turn the crank by hand and watch the valves open and close.

Chapter 2

Up and down into round

The connecting rod turns the piston's up-and-down into the crankshaft's rotation, the same way your leg turns a bicycle's pedals. One full cycle of four strokes takes two turns of the crank.

The speeds are startling. At 3,000 revolutions a minute, an ordinary motorway pace in many cars, each cylinder fires 25 times a second and each stroke takes 10 milliseconds. A piston that travels 86 mm each way averages 8.6 metres a second, stopping and reversing twice every turn.

Try it. Change the engine speed and watch the numbers. The picture stays slow so you can follow it.

Chapter 3

Why squeeze it first?

Squeezing the mixture before lighting it is what makes an engine efficient. The compression ratio is how many times smaller the space above the piston is at the top of its stroke than at the bottom.

For an ideal engine, the share of the fuel's energy turned into work depends only on that ratio: about 60% at a ratio of 10 to 1. Squeeze harder and it rises, but too hard and the mixture lights by itself before the spark, called knock, which can damage the engine.

Real engines lose heat through the cylinder walls and energy to friction. The best petrol engines now turn about 40% of their fuel into work; many older ones manage much less.

Try it. Raise the compression ratio and watch the space at the top shrink.

Chapter 4

More cylinders

One cylinder kicks once every two turns, which is rough. With four cylinders on one crankshaft, each a quarter of a cycle behind the last, one of them is always on its power stroke.

They don't fire in the order they sit. In most four-cylinder engines the order is 1, 3, 4, 2, which spreads the pushes along the crankshaft and keeps it from twisting.

Try it. Watch the red label move: 1, then 3, then 4, then 2.
