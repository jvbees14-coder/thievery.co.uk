Chapter 1

A steady wave

A radio transmitter drives electric current up and down a mast, very fast, and sends out a wave of electric and magnetic field that travels at the speed of light. On its own it is a steady hum, the , and it says nothing.

Each station has its own frequency, so a radio can pick one out. Medium-wave stations rise and fall about a million times a second; FM stations about a hundred million.

Try it. Change the frequency and watch the waves crowd together.

Chapter 2

Changing how strong it is

A voice is a much slower wobble, a few hundred to a few thousand times a second. The oldest way to send it is to let the voice set how strong the carrier is: louder when the voice pushes, weaker when it pulls. This is amplitude modulation, AM.

The receiver only has to follow the outline of the wave's peaks to get the voice back. The trouble is that a spark, a motor or lightning also changes the wave's strength, and the receiver can't tell that from the voice: it is heard as crackle.

Try it. Add interference and see it land on the voice.

Chapter 3

Changing how fast it is

Edwin Armstrong's answer, in the 1930s, was to keep the strength steady and let the voice nudge the frequency instead: slightly faster when the voice pushes, slightly slower when it pulls. FM stations swing up to 75 kHz either side of their own frequency.

The receiver listens only to the timing of the wave, so crackle that changes its strength is ignored. That is why FM sounds cleaner. Its cost is room: each station needs a wide slice of the dial.

Try it. Add the same interference and see what reaches the speaker.

Chapter 4

Long waves need tall masts

The wavelength is the speed of light divided by the frequency. An FM station at 100 MHz sends waves 3 m long; BBC Radio 4 on long wave, at 198 kHz, sends waves of 1,514 m.

An aerial works best at about a quarter of a wavelength, so an FM aerial is under a metre, while long-wave masts are hundreds of metres tall. Wi-Fi, at 2.4 GHz, has waves of 12 cm, and its aerials fit inside a phone.

Try it. Pick a station and compare its wave with a person and a mast.

Sources and notes

The waves are drawn far slower than they are: on screen the carrier makes a few dozen swings for each swing of the voice, against hundreds or thousands in a real transmitter. The receivers in chapters 2 and 3 are worked out from the drawn wave: the AM one follows the peaks, the FM one counts the time between swings.
