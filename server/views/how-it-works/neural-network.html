Chapter 1

One neuron

An artificial neuron is a very small sum. It takes some numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up with one more number called the bias, and answers yes if the total is above nought.

Feed it the two coordinates of a point, and it divides the square with a straight line: yes on one side, no on the other. The weights set the line's angle and the bias moves it along.

Try it. Move the weights until the line splits the two colours. Ringed points are on the wrong side.

Chapter 2

Learning from mistakes

Nobody needs to set the weights by hand. Show the neuron a point it gets wrong and nudge each weight a little in the direction that would have helped. Then another, and another.

This is the perceptron rule, from Frank Rosenblatt in 1958. If a straight line can split the points at all, it is certain to find one, after a number of mistakes.

Try it. Press Play, or correct one mistake at a time, and watch the line swing into place.

Chapter 3

A line isn't enough

Here one colour sits in two opposite corners and the other colour in the other two. No straight line can split them; the best manages about three-quarters.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert pointed out this limit of single neurons, and for years interest in the idea faded.

Try it. Try any line you like. Some points always end up on the wrong side.

Chapter 4

Layers

The answer is to stack them. A layer of neurons each draws its own soft line, and a final neuron combines their answers. Together they can draw curves and closed shapes.

Training needs a way to tell every weight, even deep inside, which way to move. Backpropagation does it, working backwards from the error at the output; it was made widely known in 1986. This network has just eight hidden neurons and learns either pattern in a second or two. The large models behind today's AI work the same way, with billions of weights.

Try it. Watch the network learn the ring, then the crossed pattern.
