Chapter 1

Making it up

Ask somebody to write down 100 coin tosses without tossing a coin and they will switch between heads and tails too often. Five heads in a row feels too neat to be chance, so they break it up.

Real coins don't. On the left is a made-up list; on the right, real tosses. The real one has long runs that the made-up one avoids.

Try it. Toss a new real list and compare the longest runs.

Chapter 2

How long a run to expect

In 100 tosses of a fair coin, the longest run of the same face is most often 6, and it is 6 or more 81% of the time. A list of 100 with no run longer than 4 happens less than 3% of the time.

A run is not a sign that the coin has changed. Each toss is still a half chance, and among 100 of them there are many places for a run to start.

Try it. Change the number of tosses and see how the longest run grows.

Chapter 3

Spot the fake

Knowing this, you can catch a made-up list most of the time. Count the switches: real tosses switch about half the time, a made-up list more like two times in three. Then look for the longest run.

Statistics teachers have long set this as homework: half the class tosses real coins, half invents the tosses, and the teacher sorts them at a glance.

Try it. Pick which list is real, as many times as you like.

Chapter 4

Clumps on a map

The same goes in two dimensions. Scatter 100 points at random over 100 squares and about 37 squares stay empty, while about 8 get three or more. The clumps look like they must mean something.

In the Second World War, Londoners believed the flying bombs were aimed at particular districts. In 1946 R. D. Clarke counted the hits in 576 squares of south London and found they matched the clumping chance alone would give.

Try it. Scatter new points and switch to an evenly spread version.
