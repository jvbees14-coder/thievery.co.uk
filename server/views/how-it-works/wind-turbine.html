Chapter 1

Catching moving air

Wind is moving air, and moving things carry energy. A turbine's blades are shaped like wings: the air flowing past them pushes them round, and the shaft turns a generator.

The power in the wind passing through the blades' circle depends on the air's density, the circle's area and the wind's speed. Through blades 60 m long, a 10 m/s wind carries about 7 megawatts.

Try it. Change the wind speed and watch the power.

Chapter 2

Speed, cubed

The power grows with the cube of the wind speed. Twice the speed means twice as much air through the blades each second, each bit of it carrying four times the energy: eight times the power. A 5 m/s breeze carries an eighth of what a 10 m/s wind does.

That's why turbines go on hills and out at sea, where the wind is stronger. Real turbines start making power at around 3 m/s, reach their full rating around 12, and shut down to protect themselves in gales of about 25.

Try it. Compare the power in the wind with what a typical turbine makes.

Chapter 3

You can't take it all

To take all the wind's energy, a turbine would have to stop the air dead. But stopped air would pile up behind the blades and no new air could flow through. Take too little and most of the wind passes by untouched.

In 1919 Albert Betz worked out the best balance: slow the wind to a third of its speed, and you catch 16/27 of its power, 59.3%. No turbine of any design can beat it. The best real ones catch around four fifths of that.

Try it. Find the speed behind the rotor that catches the most.

Chapter 4

Longer blades

The other way to more power is a bigger circle. Its area grows with the square of the blade length, so blades twice as long sweep four times the air.

That, and the steadier wind higher up, is why turbines keep growing. The largest offshore turbines now have blades over 100 metres long, sweeping an area the size of several football pitches.

Try it. Lengthen the blades in a 12 m/s wind.
