Chapter 1

Pressure on the move

A loudspeaker cone pushes the air in front of it, then pulls back. Each push crowds the air molecules together a little; each pull spreads them out. The crowding is passed from molecule to molecule, so a pattern of high and low pressure travels outwards.

No molecule makes the journey. Each one only shuffles back and forth around its own place, like the red one. What travels is the pattern, at about 343 metres a second in air at room temperature.

When that pattern reaches your ear, it pushes your eardrum in and out in the same rhythm, and you hear it.

Chapter 2

Pitch is frequency

How many pushes arrive each second is the frequency, measured in hertz. More pushes a second sounds higher. The A that orchestras tune to is 440 Hz.

Because the speed is fixed, a higher frequency packs the pushes closer together: the distance between them, the wavelength, is the speed divided by the frequency. At 440 Hz that is 78 cm; a deep 50 Hz hum is nearly 7 m long.

Double the frequency and you go up an octave. Each of the twelve semitones in between multiplies it by the twelfth root of 2, about 1.059.

Try it. Press Play sound (it is quiet), then slide the frequency.

Chapter 3

Why a violin doesn't sound like a flute

A pure tone is a smooth sine wave, and sounds dull. A string or a column of air in an instrument vibrates at its main frequency and, at the same time, at two, three, four times it and more. These extra tones are .

The ear hears them as one note, but the mix of harmonics gives the note its character, its timbre. Same pitch, different recipe.

Chapter 4

Any sound from sine waves

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any repeating wave can be built by adding sine waves. Even a square wave, all sharp corners, is the sum of the odd harmonics, each smaller in proportion: 1, ⅓, ⅕, ⅐ and on.

Each term brings the sum closer, but a small overshoot at every corner never goes away: about 9% of the jump, however many terms you add. It is called the Gibbs phenomenon.

Try it. Add harmonics one at a time and listen as the tone turns buzzy.

Chapter 5

What we can hear

Young human ears hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The top end falls with age, and many adults hear little above about 15,000 Hz.

A piano covers 27.5 Hz to 4,186 Hz, but its notes carry harmonics far above that, and without them it would not sound like a piano.

Try it. Play the sound and slide to the top and the bottom until you can no longer hear it. Small speakers give out well before 20 Hz.
