Chapter 1

A curve that isn't there

Roll a ball straight across a turning roundabout. To someone standing beside it, the ball goes in a straight line. To someone riding the roundabout, it curves away, as if pushed sideways by a force.

Nothing pushes it: the rider is turning underneath it. But for anyone on the turning thing, it is simplest to describe as a sideways push, and it has a name: the Coriolis force, after Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who worked it out in 1835.

Try it. Watch the roll from the room, then ride the table.

Chapter 2

On a spinning planet

We all ride a turning roundabout: the Earth, once a day. Anything moving a long way over its surface, air or ocean water, drifts to the right of its path in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.

The effect is strongest at the poles and vanishes at the equator. It is tiny over a short distance, but over hours and hundreds of kilometres it bends paths a great deal.

Try it. Change the latitude and watch how far the path bends, with nothing in the way to stop it.

Chapter 3

Why storms turn

Air flows towards areas of low pressure. In the northern hemisphere, all of it is nudged to the right as it goes, so instead of flowing straight in it swirls round the low anticlockwise. In the southern hemisphere, clockwise.

That is why every hurricane in the north turns one way and every cyclone in the south the other. Hurricanes can't form right on the equator, where there is no turn to start them spinning.

Try it. Switch hemisphere and watch the air spiral the other way.

Chapter 4

Not your bath

It is often said that baths and sinks drain one way in each hemisphere. They don't, or not for this reason. Over a basin's few seconds and centimetres, the Earth's turning gives water a push nearly a million times weaker than gravity, and about a hundred times weaker than a gentle swirl of a centimetre a second left over from filling it.

Whatever swirl the water starts with wins. Only with great care, such as Ascher Shapiro's experiment of 1962, letting a large tank settle for a day before draining it, does the Earth's spin decide the direction.
