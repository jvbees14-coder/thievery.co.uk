Chapter 1

Fifty voters, five seats

A small country has 50 voters: 30 vote purple and 20 vote gold, 60% to 40%. It is split into five districts of ten voters, and each district elects whoever gets most of its votes.

Somebody has to decide where the district lines go. That choice turns out to matter as much as the votes.

Try it. Look at where each party's voters live.

Chapter 2

Same votes, different lines

Draw the districts as five rows and every row has six purple voters and four gold: purple wins all five seats. Draw them as pairs of columns and purple wins three, gold two, close to the share of the vote.

Both maps are neat rectangles, and both are honestly drawn. Nobody has changed a vote.

Try it. Switch between the two plans.

Chapter 3

Packing and cracking

Now draw the lines on purpose. Pack as many purple voters as possible into two districts, which purple wins ten to nothing, wasting votes it didn't need. Crack the rest of them thinly across three districts, where gold wins each by six to four or more.

Gold, with 40% of the vote, now wins three seats out of five. This is , named after Elbridge Gerry, governor of Massachusetts, whose party drew a district in 1812 that a newspaper said looked like a salamander.

Try it. Compare the packed plan with the fair ones.

Chapter 4

Counting wasted votes

One way to spot it is to count wasted votes: every vote for a loser, and every winning vote beyond the half needed. The difference between the two parties' wasted votes, as a share of all votes, is the . In the packed plan purple wastes 20 votes and gold 5: a gap of 30% against purple.

No single number settles it; even the tidy column plan has a gap of 10%. That is why some countries, the UK among them, hand the drawing to independent commissions with rules about equal numbers and keeping communities together.
