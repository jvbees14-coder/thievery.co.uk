Chapter 1

A snowflake of bumps

Start with a triangle. On the middle third of every edge, add a smaller triangle pointing out. Now do the same to every edge of the new shape, and again, for ever. Helge von Koch described this snowflake in 1904.

Each step the edges multiply by four. Zoom into any part and it looks like the whole: the same bumps on bumps at every scale. A shape like that is called a fractal.

Try it. Add steps and watch the edge crinkle.

Chapter 2

An endless edge round a finite area

Every step replaces each edge with four pieces a third as long, making the outline 4/3 as long as before. Keep going and it grows without limit: after ten steps it's nearly 18 times the triangle's, after a hundred, over three thousand billion times.

Yet the area barely changes. The added triangles shrink so fast that the total never passes 8/5 of the original triangle. An infinitely long boundary round a small, fixed area.

Try it. Compare how the edge and the area grow with each step.

Chapter 3

Measuring a coast

Real coastlines are a bit like this: bays within bays, rocks within coves. Lewis Fry Richardson noticed that the length you measure depends on your ruler. Walk a pair of dividers along a map with a long span and you skip every inlet; shorten it and the coast seems to grow.

There's no settled answer to how long a coast is, only an answer for a given ruler. That's why published lengths for the same coastline can differ a great deal.

Try it. Shorten the ruler and watch the measured length climb.

Chapter 4

Between a line and a plane

Plot the measured length against the ruler, both on logarithmic scales, and the points fall on a straight line. Its slope says how quickly the coast keeps revealing detail. Benoit Mandelbrot turned that into a number in 1967, in a paper called "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?": the fractal dimension.

A smooth line has dimension 1 and a filled area 2. The Koch snowflake's edge is about 1.26. Richardson's figures for Britain's west coast give about 1.25, and this made-up coast comes out close to that.

Sources and notes

The snowflake and the made-up coast are drawn in your browser; the coast is built by adding random bumps at every scale. Its dimension is one minus the slope of the best straight line through the points in chapter 4.
