Chapter 1

A spring and a problem

A mechanical watch runs on a coiled steel ribbon, the mainspring, wound by turning the crown or by a weight that swings as you move. Through a train of gears, its pull turns the hands.

Left to itself, a spring lets go all at once. Its energy has to come out in tiny, equal portions, and something has to decide how often.

Try it. Take away the escapement and see how long the spring lasts.

Chapter 2

The balance wheel

That something is the balance: a small wheel on a fine spiral spring, the hairspring. Turn it and the hairspring pulls it back, so it swings to and fro like a pendulum that doesn't need gravity, and so works in any position.

How fast it swings depends on the wheel's weight and the spring's stiffness, not on how far it swings. Most modern watches run at 4 Hz: four complete there-and-back swings every second, which is eight single swings, or beats, a second and 28,800 an hour. Each swing turns the wheel through about 270°.

Chapter 3

The escapement

The escapement links the swinging balance to the spring-driven gears. It has two parts: a toothed escape wheel, pushed round by the spring, and a lever called the pallet fork, which holds it.

Each time the balance swings through the middle, a pin on it knocks the fork across. That lets the escape wheel forward by half a tooth, and the tooth pushes the fork as it goes, giving the balance a tiny kick to keep it swinging. Then the fork locks the wheel again.

The tick you hear is this: one beat, one step. The escape wheel can only move as fast as the balance allows.

Try it. Slow it right down and watch the fork flick each time the balance passes the middle.

Chapter 4

From ticks to time

From there it is gear ratios. The escape wheel's steps drive a wheel that turns once a minute and carries the seconds hand; further gears divide by 60 for the minute hand and by 12 again for the hour hand.

That's why a mechanical seconds hand seems to sweep: at 28,800 beats an hour it moves in eight small steps every second. A quartz watch's hand jumps once a second.

Chapter 5

How good is it?

A balance's rate shifts a little with temperature, with which way up the watch is, and as the spring runs down. Watches certified as chronometers by COSC, the Swiss testing office, must average between 4 seconds slow and 6 seconds fast a day, tested in several positions and temperatures.

That sounds tiny, but it adds up: at +6 seconds a day a watch is three minutes fast after a month. A quartz watch, whose crystal vibrates 32,768 times a second, is usually far closer.

Try it. Set a daily rate and run a month.
