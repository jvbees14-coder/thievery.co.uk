Chapter 1

Teeth for teeth

When you turn the pedals once, the big chainring at the front pulls its number of teeth of chain round: 50 teeth of chain, say. At the back, the chain turns the small sprocket on the wheel, and every time 17 teeth pass, a 17-tooth sprocket goes round once.

So one turn of the pedals turns the back wheel 50 ÷ 17, nearly three times. With a road wheel 2.1 m round, that's about 6.2 m of road for each turn of your legs.

Try it. Change the chainring and the sprocket, and watch how far each turn takes you.

Chapter 2

Distance or force

Nothing comes free. A gear that turns the wheel further for each turn of the pedals also needs a harder push on them. The work your legs do is the same either way; a gear only trades distance for force.

Climbing an 8% hill, about 85 kg of rider and bike needs an average push on the pedals of nearly 600 newtons in the biggest gear, like lifting 60 kg with each stroke. In the smallest gear it's under 150, about a quarter, but you pedal four times as many turns to climb the same hill.

Try it. Make the hill steeper and find a gear you could push.

Chapter 3

Keep the legs turning

Legs work best in a fairly narrow range of pace. Racing cyclists usually pedal at around 80 to 100 turns a minute, and change gear to keep that pace whatever the road does, rather than pedalling slowly and hard.

At 90 turns a minute, the same legs move this bike at about 13 km/h in its lowest gear and over 50 km/h in its highest. The gears are what let one comfortable pace cover all of it.

Try it. Set a pace and change gear to see the speed it gives.

Chapter 4

Twenty-two gears, fewer really

A road bike with two chainrings and eleven sprockets has 22 combinations. But the two rows overlap: much of what the small ring does, the big ring does too, with a different sprocket.

Lined up by distance, about 19 of the 22 are noticeably different from their neighbours. Cyclists also avoid crossing the chain from the big ring to the biggest sprocket, or the small ring to the smallest, because the chain runs at an angle and wears faster.

Try it. Pick a gear and find its near twin on the other ring.

Sources and notes

The bike has 50 and 34-tooth chainrings, an 11 to 30-tooth cassette, 172.5 mm cranks and a 700×25 tyre 2.105 m round. The pushes on the pedals are averages over a turn, for climbing slowly, ignoring air and rolling resistance. "Noticeably different" means more than 3% apart.
