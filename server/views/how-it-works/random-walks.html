Chapter 1

A coin for every step

Toss a coin. Heads, take a step up; tails, a step down. Do it again and again. This is a random walk, and it turns up everywhere: a gambler's winnings, a share price, a speck of dust jostled by molecules.

The walk has no memory and no pull back to the start. It wanders off in long runs that look like trends, though each step is a fresh toss.

Try it. Watch a walk, then start again. Each one looks as if it is going somewhere.

Chapter 2

The square root

Start five hundred walkers together. On average they stay centred on the start, since up and down are equally likely. But they spread out, and how far grows with the square root of the number of steps.

After 100 steps a typical walker is about 10 steps from home; after 400, about 20. Four times the time, only twice the distance. That's why a drop of ink spreads quickly at first and then ever more slowly through still water.

Try it. Watch the crowd spread, and the dashed lines move out at the square root of the steps.

Chapter 3

The gambler's walk

A gambler with £10 bets £1 at a time and stops when they have doubled it or lost it all. With a fair coin they double their money half the time. On a roulette wheel, betting on red, the small edge for the casino tilts every step, and the chance of doubling falls to 37%.

The more steps the walk takes, the more the tilt tells. A gambler starting with £100 and aiming for £200 doubles it only about one time in two hundred.

Try it. Play the forty gamblers out, then switch to a fair coin and compare.

Chapter 4

Will it come home?

On a flat grid, the walker steps north, south, east or west at random. In 1921 George Pólya proved that a walk like this, in one or two dimensions, is certain to come back to where it started eventually, though it may take a very long time.

In three dimensions it isn't. A walker in space returns home only about a third of the time, and otherwise wanders off for good.

Try it. Watch the trail, and count how often it passes the green start.
