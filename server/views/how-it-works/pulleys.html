Chapter 1

The lever

Put a plank over a pivot with a heavy crate on the short end. Push down on the long end and the crate rises. The plank balances when the push times its distance from the pivot equals the load times its distance.

So with the pivot a fifth of the way along, your side is four times as long, and a 100 kg crate needs a push like lifting just 25 kg. Archimedes, who worked this out over two thousand years ago, is said to have boasted that with a long enough lever he could move the world.

Try it. Move the pivot and watch the push needed.

Chapter 2

One pulley

A rope over a single fixed pulley gives no help with the weight: to hold up 100 kg you still pull as hard as lifting 100 kg. What it does is change the direction. Pulling down is easier than lifting up, because you can use your own weight.

Chapter 3

Block and tackle

Now hang the load from a second, moving block of pulleys, and run the rope up and down between the two blocks several times. One rope carries the same pull all along its length, so each run that holds up the moving block shares the weight.

With four runs holding the load, each takes a quarter, and you pull with the force of 25 kg to hold 100. Sailors and builders have used this for thousands of years.

Try it. Add rope runs and watch the pull fall.

Chapter 4

Nothing for nothing

The catch: to raise the load one metre, each of the four runs must shorten by a metre, so you pull four metres of rope. A quarter of the force, four times the distance.

Force times distance is the work done, and it comes out the same at both ends. Every lever, pulley and gear trades force for distance in the same way; none can give out more work than goes in, and real ones lose some to friction.

Try it. Pull the rope and compare the work in with the work out.

Sources and notes
