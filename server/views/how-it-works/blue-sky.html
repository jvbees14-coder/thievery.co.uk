Chapter 1

Light meets air

Sunlight is a mix of every colour. On its way through the air, a little of it hits gas molecules and is bounced off in a new direction. This is called scattering.

Molecules are far smaller than a wavelength of light, and for anything that small, short wavelengths are scattered much more than long ones: in proportion to one over the wavelength to the fourth power. Blue light is scattered about six times as much as red. Lord Rayleigh worked this out in 1871.

Try it. Watch the blue and violet rays get knocked aside while the red carries on.

Chapter 2

The glow overhead

Look at any part of the sky away from the Sun and you are seeing scattered sunlight: light that was heading elsewhere until a molecule sent it towards your eye. Since that light is mostly the short wavelengths, the sky looks blue.

Why not violet, which scatters even more? Sunlight holds less violet than blue to begin with, and our eyes are much less sensitive to it. The mix of scattered light that reaches us looks sky blue.

Chapter 3

Red sunsets

When the Sun is low, its light comes to you sideways through the atmosphere, through about 38 times as much air as when it is overhead. Over that long path nearly all the blue is scattered away, out of the beam.

What reaches you straight from the Sun is mostly the longer wavelengths: yellow, orange and red. Dust and smoke in the air scatter more still, which is why sunsets after volcanic eruptions or wildfires can be especially red.

Try it. Lower the Sun and watch the blue bar shrink.

Chapter 4

White clouds

Clouds are made of water droplets thousands of times bigger than air molecules, much bigger than a wavelength of light. Particles that size scatter every colour about equally.

So a cloud sends back the whole mix of sunlight, and the whole mix looks white. Thick clouds look grey underneath only because less light gets all the way through them.

Try it. Make the particles bigger and watch the colours even out.

Sources and notes

The shares in chapter 3 are for air molecules alone, using the atmosphere's measured Rayleigh optical depth (0.097 at 550 nm) and Kasten and Young's formula for the length of the path; dust, haze and ozone take more. In chapter 4 the change from molecules to droplets is shown as a smooth slide, a simplification of the real physics of large particles.
