Chapter 1

Always half lit

The Moon makes no light of its own. The Sun lights it, and at any moment exactly half of it is lit: the half facing the Sun.

What changes through the month is how much of that lit half faces us. Between us and the Sun, the Moon shows us its dark side, and we see nothing: a new moon. On the far side of us, it shows us the whole lit face: a full moon.

Try it. Move the Moon round, or press Play. The lit half always faces the Sun.

Chapter 2

Crescent, quarter, gibbous, full

A week after new moon, the Moon is a quarter of the way round and we see half its face lit: the first quarter. Then it grows, or waxes, through gibbous to full, and shrinks, or wanes, back again.

It isn't the Earth's shadow. That is a common mix-up: the Earth's shadow only falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which happens at full moon, and only now and then.

Try it. Compare each point of the orbit with the face we see from there.

Chapter 3

A month of 29½ days

The Moon goes once round the Earth, measured against the stars, in 27.32 days. But a full cycle of phases takes 29.53 days.

The difference is the Earth moving. In those 27 days the Earth has carried the Moon about a thirteenth of the way round the Sun, so the Sun is now in a different direction. The Moon needs about two more days to catch up and line up with it again for the next new moon.

Try it. Jump to one turn, then to the next new moon, and compare the two arrows.

Chapter 4

Why not an eclipse every month?

At every new moon the Moon is between us and the Sun. So why doesn't it hide the Sun every month? Because its orbit is tilted by about 5° to the Earth's. Most months the new moon passes above or below the line to the Sun, by as much as 34,000 km.

The two orbits cross in two places. Only when a new moon falls near one of those crossings does its shadow touch the Earth. That happens in two seasons a year, so there are between two and five solar eclipses every year, each seen from only a narrow strip of the planet.

Try it. Step through two years of new moons and find the ones that line up.

Sources and notes

The orbits in the scene are circles, not to scale. In chapter 4 a new moon counts as an eclipse when it falls within 16.5° of a crossing, the middle of the real limit, which runs from about 15.4° to 18.5° because the Moon's distance varies.
