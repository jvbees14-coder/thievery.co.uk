Chapter 1

A fridge the other way round

A fridge pumps heat out of a cold box into a warm kitchen; you can feel it at the back. A heat pump does the same thing to the outdoors: it takes heat out of the cold air and pumps it into the house.

The heat is carried by a refrigerant that goes round and round a sealed loop. It soaks up heat outside, is squeezed by a compressor until it is hotter than the radiators, gives its heat away indoors, and is let out through a valve to go round again.

Try it. Follow the refrigerant round and watch its temperature change.

Chapter 2

Boiling below freezing

How can air at 7 °C warm anything? The trick is a liquid that boils at a very low temperature. Propane, used in many new heat pumps, boils at −42 °C at ordinary pressure. Even freezing air is warm enough to boil it, and boiling soaks up a great deal of heat.

Squeeze a gas and it gets hotter, and it takes a higher temperature to boil it. At about 17 times the air's pressure, propane boils at 50 °C. So in the indoor coil, at high pressure, it turns back into liquid and gives up the heat it soaked up outside.

Try it. Change the pressure and see where propane boils.

Chapter 3

More heat out than electricity in

An electric heater turns each unit of electricity into one unit of heat. A heat pump uses its electricity to move heat that was already outdoors, so it delivers more heat than it uses. On a mild day, three units of heat or more for each unit of electricity.

That ratio is called the coefficient of performance. It doesn't break any law of physics: the extra heat isn't made, it's moved.

Chapter 4

Colder days, hotter radiators

The bigger the jump in temperature, from the outdoor air to the radiator water, the harder the pump has to work. So it is least efficient on the coldest days, when it is needed most, though it still gives out more heat than it takes in.

The other half of the jump is the radiators. A system that heats water to 35 °C, with bigger radiators or underfloor heating, does much better than one that has to reach 55 °C. In a British field trial, air-source heat pumps averaged about 2.5 units of heat per unit of electricity over a year.

Try it. Make it colder outside and the radiators hotter, and watch the figure fall.

Sources and notes
