Chapter 1

Too many ones

Take a big collection of real-world numbers, such as town populations, river lengths or company accounts, and look only at the first digit of each. You might expect each of 1 to 9 to turn up about one time in nine.

They don't. A 1 leads about 30% of the time, a 2 about 18%, and a 9 under 5%. The pattern is called Benford's law, after the physicist who studied it in 1938, though Simon Newcomb noticed it in 1881 from the worn first pages of books of logarithms.

Chapter 2

Why 1 wins

Think of something growing steadily, say by 10% a year. To get from 100 to 200 it must double. To get from 900 to 1,000 it needs to grow only 11%. So it spends much longer with a leading 1 than with a leading 9.

On a scale where every tenfold rise takes the same room, the stretch from 1 to 2 fills 30% of it and the stretch from 9 to 10 under 5%. Numbers that grow by percentages, or span many powers of ten, fall into each stretch in proportion to its width.

Chapter 3

It turns up everywhere

Powers of 2, the Fibonacci numbers and money growing at interest all follow the law closely. So do many messier collections: populations, prices, stock volumes and physical constants.

It doesn't apply to everything. Numbers held in a narrow range, such as adult heights in centimetres, or numbers assigned by rule, such as phone numbers, ignore it.

Chapter 4

Catching made-up numbers

People inventing figures tend to spread their digits evenly, because that feels random. The result looks nothing like Benford's law.

Accountants and tax investigators use this as a warning sign: a set of expense claims or accounts whose first digits are too even deserves a closer look. It proves nothing on its own, but it tells them where to dig.
